Locating the Written Word

December 1, 2025

Written for Wittgenstein and Literary Theory at Duke, Fall 2025.

In Chapter 9 of Revolution of the Ordinary, Moi writes, “Written down, discourse escapes its original temporal situation, addresses a universal audience, and no longer refers, but rather, in Heidegger’s term, ‘discloses’ a world” (Moi, 199).

At the end of the same chapter, she mentions, “The ‘author’s intentions,’ then are imagined to reside outside the text, and to precede it as cause to effect. An intention becomes an ethereal object in the mind, a matter of the author’s psychology. This mental phenomenon is imagined to cause the work, pictured as an atemporal object with clear spacial boundaries” (Moi, 200).

It is interesting that the discourse escapes its original temporal situation while the picture of the author’s intentions, even described as atemporal, does not escape in the same way, not causing outside of time and traveling with the written discourse. It is hard for me to detach from the picture of the written word as an object where the intention “precedes and causes it” (Moi, 203). Even if the intention is ungraspable, lost in time, I still see it as there being created in the moment of history when the author is writing whatever work, and still relevant past that moment in relation to the audience of the final product. I guess, in reframing the written work as an action, the intention is only kept alive by the written word. If the written word was not read, or was destroyed, then the intention, or audience’s craving for an intention, would be destroyed with it, as there is no use. Similarly, if an author had an intention that was not realized in the written word, then it would never be discussed or used, because the intention is not relevant to the work. The only direction to inquire about the intention is backward from the work. With this, the intentions cannot be found completely divorced from the work. But in this entire picture of intention, I still fall into the gap model where, even if the intention is enmeshed and deeply related to the written word, it still exists as an “atemporal” and “ethereal object in the mind.”

Moi helps to further dispel this picture through Cavell’s words, that there is no difference between “what is intended” and “what is there” (Moi, 203). As a writer, I sometimes intend to convey certain themes through my work, only to get feedback from a group workshop and realize they are not present in the text. Still, I feel like my intention, or desire, is real, even if unrealized in the text. While this unrealized intention is not relevant for literary criticism, it is hard to detach from a hope during creation and treat it as useless. Seeing this as a main block for me in clearing the fog to understand Moi’s picture of intention, I think I can get there if I take my unrealized (usually badly formed) ideas as less concrete and meaningful just because I thought of them.
