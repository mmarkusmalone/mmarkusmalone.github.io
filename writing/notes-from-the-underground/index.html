Notes from the Underground

February 1, 2025

Written for Existentialism at Duke, Spring 2025.

What struck me when reading Notes from the Underground was the sadness and anxiety that I took on for the narrator. Dostoevsky’s writing works to elicit these emotions from the reader through operative details. One of my favorite passages was from the aftermath of the meeting with his old schoolmates:

“The disorder, the leftovers, a broken glass on the floor, spilled wine, cigarette butts, drunkenness and delirium in my head agonizing torment in my heart” (57).

The collage of images that Dostoevsky pieces together to describe the mental state of the narrator is genius. It deeply impacted me in its overall picture of loneliness and lameness. I also loved the image of the narrator being bumped by the officer, where the officer pretended that he did not exist. Dostoevsky crafted the scene and the insignificance of the narrator so well, I felt true second-hand embarrassment.

I am curious as to what Dostoevsky is trying to communicate with this tone. To what end does he evoke this sympathy? My sympathies are through the normative societal lens of what people should be embarrassed and sad about. But the narrator often says he should be really embarrassed and doesn’t admit to actually being embarrassed. One idea could be that the narrator does not align with the ideals of his time (the true, good, and beautiful), and by calling attention to a character whose motivations and feelings are incomprehensible to a general audience, Dostoevsky is highlighting the existence of such desires and motivations outside of the bounds of normative societal ideals. But still, he attempts to gain respect from society and suffers in his failure to achieve that. I am really not sure.
