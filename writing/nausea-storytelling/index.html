Nausea: Storytelling

March 2, 2025

Written for Existentialism at Duke, Spring 2025.

I was drawn to Sartre’s exploration of storytelling: the relationship between our experiences and how we contextualize them in this framework. He writes, “And naturally, everything they tell about books can happen in real life, but not in the same way. It is to this way of happening that I clung so tightly” (37). He was referring to his “adventures” abroad, and how they lose meaning without the visibility and validity of storytelling. I was curious about how the storytelling, narrative framework shapes how we anticipate common human experiences. I feel like the experience of love is especially limited by this. There are endless TV shows, movies, and books that curate stories we look to for inspiration of what kind of relationship we want: what meet-cute, what wedding, what tiny intimate moments we share with our partners. I think that people “cling” to this “way of happening” in the same way that Sartre illuminates, forcing their own experience of love to mimic the narrative of a rom-com and being disappointed when it doesn’t have the same “cute” factor. I think this works to make relationships less authentic in one way by directing too much energy into the superficial aspects of them, like how the narrative sounds to others. Another way is that even the most intimate moments, like sex, become objects for storytelling, reducing them to curated acts. Still, I don’t think outside influence always takes away from authenticity within love. Our definitions of love are shaped by so many other outside forces (family, friends, our own experiences) in meaningful ways. I think that the framework of storytelling works to disempower the ugly, authentic, and intimate parts innate to the experience of love.
