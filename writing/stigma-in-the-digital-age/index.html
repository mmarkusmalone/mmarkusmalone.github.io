Stigma in the Digital Age

April 26, 2024

Written for Bad Behavior at Duke, Spring 2024.

In Stigma, Erving Goffman analyzes how behavior is rooted in interactions and educates even the deepest parts of one’s identity. He breaks down identity into three subsets: the social, personal, and ego. These work together to define oneself and manage behavior according to that definition. Despite being published in 1963, the concepts introduced in Stigma are still relevant to the dynamics of today’s world. Joshua Meyrowitz’s analysis of the unique qualities of electronic media that affect humans in No Sense of Place works to illuminate how certain concepts in Stigma manifest differently in the digital age. The form of electronic media, as described by Meyrowitz, undermines the basic unit of society and disrupts information management introduced by Goffman by making it possible to have one-sided and asynchronous interactions in which one’s identities are affected.

For Goffman, the unit of society is made up of two people coming together in competition for who is normal and who is not. Due to the advent of electronic media, this unit is undermined by the base units of interaction available to a solitary individual. Meyrowitz writes, “A wide spectrum of behavior is conveyed through electronic media. Emotions and feelings ‘leak out’” (Meyrowitz, 114). Through the “leaking out” of feelings, Meyrowitz provokes the idea that one can have a behavioral interaction with the television. A private interaction, in one’s home alone for example, occurs with the public realm in the television. This interaction is one-sided, making it more basic than two people coming together, which gives way to multiple “units” of society that can impact how people behave and relate to their identities.

One way this interaction manifests is through the “stare.” Meyrowitz elaborates, “In live interactions, a person cannot devote all his or her energy to monitoring the behavior of others because he or she is also deeply involved both in producing expressions and communications and in thinking about how other people are monitoring those behaviors. But with television, the viewer can slump in a chair and focus exclusively on the expressive behavior of those televised” (Meyrowitz, 101). A behavioral interaction between a solitary person and a television, through the “stare,” can affect one’s social and ego identity in a manner different from the effects through Goffman’s “unit of society” and the building blocks upon that unit. When a viewer stares, all of their attention is devoted to analyzing the behavior of the television, which leads to heightened reflection and awareness of stigma dynamics playing out in front of them. For example, one’s social identity can be affected by watching a comedy show such as Curb Your Enthusiasm or stand-up comics in a similar vein. In Curb Your Enthusiasm, the main character Larry David often picks on off-putting, usually harmless, habits and has an explosive reaction for comic effect. In one episode, Larry David realizes that the customer in front of him is abusing the ice cream free samples and yells at her. While it was funny, I was staring at the TV and identified with the stigmatized free-sampler, beginning to wonder if people have been judging me for getting multiple testers. My social identity was modified in that my awareness of whether I was in the stigmatized or normal group in the context of an ice cream shop shifted in a way that has not been corrected since. Ego identity also can be affected by these shows, as I negotiate whether or not to ask for testers according to newfound shame for the behavior: shame sparked by Larry David. Comics often make fun of niche behavior, calling out a portion of the viewers at home and affecting their social and ego identity. The idea that viewers’ behavior can be modified not only by people they interact with face-to-face, but by people on the screen whom a viewer will probably never come into contact with or be personally stigmatized by, modifies the bidirectional nature of interaction developed by Goffman. The effects of the “stare” and similar solitary interactions with electronic media add layers, previously unexplored by Goffman, to the way that social and ego identity can be affected by interaction.

The functionality and process of information management as laid out by Goffman is also complicated by less controllable expressions put forth by Meyrowitz. In any behavioral interaction, one is managing social signs according to their ego identity. Goffman writes that we choose “To display or not to display; to tell or not to tell; to let on or not to let on; to lie or not to lie; and in each case, to whom, how, when, and where” (Goffman, 42). We do this in part through the management of disidentifiers, defined as “a sign that tends—in fact or hope—to break up an otherwise coherent picture but in this case in a positive direction desired by the actor, not so much establishing a new claim as throwing severe doubt on the validity of the virtual one” (Goffman, 44). Disidentifiers can manifest in the form of wearing certain clothing, talking in a certain way, and even having a “wig” to hide an aspect of one’s identity that is stigmatized. They are tools for information management that work to persuade others of one’s ideal identity. However, the credibility of one’s chosen disidentifiers can be fractured by one’s expressions. Meyrowitz writes, “One can start and stop communicating at will, but one cannot stop expressing. Communications are consciously given, but expressions are unconsciously ‘given off’” (Meyrowitz, 94). Disidentifiers are products of communication, as they are active choices people make to manage how people judge them, but expressions such as the tone of one’s voice or body language impact an impression to a much higher degree, as Meyrowitz cites anthropologist Albert Mehrabian’s studies of nonverbal behavior, which conclude that 90% of the meaning of a message is derived from expressions (Meyrowitz, 100). In interactions between people in person, expressions are totally visible and work to undermine communicative information management techniques such as the employment of disidentifiers, as there is no filter to unconscious expression.

In electronic media, there exist varying degrees of filtering reality and expressions, which work to modify the previously laid out mechanisms of information management. Meyrowitz writes, “The more the medium simply ‘captures’ what is happening in a situation, without any particular effort on the part of those present, the more messages will closely resemble the behavior they are about. And the more messages are tied to real behavior and events, the less they can be manipulated and controlled” (Meyrowitz, 109). While behavioral interactions can take place through electronic media, they can be filtered to various degrees, which distorts expressions and therefore the interactions. The filtration of digital media lends itself to a newfound widespread and unconscious use of disidentifiers and social symbols. The widespread use of Photoshop, and, less dramatically, more flattering angles, works to throw severe doubt on one’s virtual identity of potential disloyalty to certain beauty standards. This is more severe than makeup or a pretty outfit, as behavioral interactions in person reveal cakiness of heavy foundation or tightness of a too-small dress that are uncontrollable. These expressions can easily be completely filtered by actual photo filters or even the nature of the stillness of a photo. The mass use of disidentifiers to control how one’s appearance is being perceived turns every flattering Instagram poster into a discreditable figure. Their cover can be broken any time they are seen in person in any light or angle that fails to live up to the advertised version of themselves. People can be completely normal and still discreditable because of the filtration in electronic media where a lot of interaction takes place.

For electronic media with less filtration, like live news or being publicized on electronic media without one’s knowledge, one has a similar control of information management techniques as in in-person interactions, but less grasp over “known-aboutness.” The “known-aboutness” of a stigma depends on the actual visibility of the stigma along with “whether or not they have previous knowledge about him—and this can be based on gossip about him or a previous contact with him during which his stigma was visible” (Goffman, 49). When interactions were mostly limited to in person, the known-aboutness could be affected by previous contacts, gossip, and what is known about a person in specific circles. While information could still spread, it was localized to the physical boundaries and settings. With electronic media, these boundaries fall away, which means that one has less of a grasp on the “known-aboutness” of a certain stigma. Someone can post an unflattering photo or private information about anyone else for an audience—large or small, local or global—with equal ease. This kind of information is formatted in pixels and links which can be saved offline and copied. It is more concrete than words and hearsay. One’s electronic identity is ungraspable, which leaves people today in the dark about what information people have about them and how to manage that information.

The internet is the venue where the new generation of social interaction and behavior takes place. With this modified space, where words are communicated through bits and pictures in pixels, there emerge changes in manifestations of information management and identity. With this evolution, identities and stigmas can be defined by TikTok microtrends and influencers who are 90% photoshopped and marketed to please the masses. One simultaneously micromanages their image and has no grasp on what people know about them on the internet. The internet changes who one is, how they act and think about themselves, and what they look to be. The digital is deeply seeped into the new generation’s identity.
