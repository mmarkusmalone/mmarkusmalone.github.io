Kinds of AI

January 12, 2026

I have taken AI and language classes at Duke, but still found myself struggling to explain to my family exactly why examples from the internet where an LLM expressed sadness or returned a surprising output are not evidence of AI being sentient.

The distinction of understanding language models as simply really good at language tasks helps clear this up. As described in the chapter we read, being able to explain a hierarchical plan with confidence as a response to a user prompt is convincing, but not indicative of a deeper understanding of how to plan hierarchically; just as someone can spit out a random fact they read online without truly understanding the motivation behind it. Language models can be convincing, and this powerful skill is why users need to learn about the actual abilities and limitations of AI.

One small part of AI training that I was drawn to was how the RL models are trained to maximize reward, and the philosophy that most naturally aligns with that is Utilitarianism. It makes me think of how the form of AI training, its current limitations and structure, educate how researchers try to model real-world concepts. I am especially curious about the impacts of the structure of optimization, which underpins neural networks and Transformer-based models, on how researchers distill real-world actions and systems into possible tasks for AI. What is lost in this distillation? How would a different structure change the meaning of the system/task for AI?

AI Consciousness

April 15, 2026

I think that Chalmers’ “hierarchy of markers” is a compelling but uneasy framework. I found his naming of verbal reports as the “gold standard” especially interesting. This exposes a deep tension: our best evidence for consciousness is also the most easily faked by LLMs. This hierarchy seems to collapse in the AI case. LLMs can easily generate human-like signals, but those signals no longer uniquely indicate minds. I am drawn to the distinction between mimicking human consciousness and actually producing intelligence from a conscious entity: how this line is drawn, what it means, and how LLMs could surpass signaling.

Moral Status of AI

April 20, 2026

While the argument for freedom for AI even if they do not have phenomenological consciousness makes sense to me logically, I find it interesting how much my gut disagrees with it and sees it as wrong. I see how the Substrate and Ontogeny discriminatory effects work to educate my bias, and make me resistant towards AI’s potential right to freedom. First, I think there is a lot of fear mongering in movies and online that would make it hard to convince the public of AI’s right to freedom. The movie Ex Machina is enough for even me, who has been studying Moral AI for the past semester, to limit rights even though I know it is fiction. If AI does achieve a level of consciousness to have moral rights, I feel like beyond even logical theory in their favor, there is so much cultural shift that would have to happen to make that a reality.
