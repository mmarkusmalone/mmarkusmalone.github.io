Lord Chandos’ Beetle

February 23, 2025

Written for Existentialism at Duke, Spring 2025.

In describing an almost manic state of ecstasy where meaning is everywhere, Hofmannsthal writes, “At these moments an insignificant creature, a dog, a rat, a beetle, a stunted apple tree, a cart path winding over the hill, a moss-covered stone mean more to me than the most beautiful, most abandoned lover ever did on the happiest night” (125). My reaction to this description was thinking that the mentality was absurd, manic, and obviously signaling a disconnect from reality. But upon further reflection, I am curious as to why I attribute so much more inherent meaning to a memory than to any random bug. The example that Hofmannsthal uses, the memory of an “abandoned lover,” is a rich base for this kind of contemplation. That memory has inherent sentimentality, with powerful emotions of love and longing attached to it. In the most profound cases, it could have contributed to an aspect of personal identity or concept of love. But still, a memory of an “abandoned lover” is stale because it is evoking something dead, not active and full of life. It is not contributing to an end in the present. Despite this, the power of the emotions we attach to it makes it “meaningful” for us. It seems like this is the framework for a lot of memories, where the “meaning” lies in the strength of the emotions attached to them. But emotions are random and fantastic. I don’t think there lies inherent meaning within the power of emotions, because a hormone imbalance can tap you into the depth of those emotions over absolutely nothing. A beetle is contributing to the functioning of the ecosystem and has inherent purpose in its life. Why is it weird for the life of a passing beetle to hold more meaning than a stale memory? For Lord Chandos, this breakdown of hierarchy in meaning makes it impossible for him to live normally. But is he less sane than the “normal” people who are unconsciously devoted to this hierarchy?
