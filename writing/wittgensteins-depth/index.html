Wittgenstein’s “Depth”

September 13, 2025

Written for Wittgenstein and Literary Theory at Duke, Fall 2025.

§111: The problems arising through a misinterpretation of our forms of language have the characteristics of depth. They are deep disquietudes; they are as deeply rooted in us as the forms of our language, and their significance is as great as the importance of our language. — Let’s ask ourselves: why do we feel a grammatical joke to be deep? (And that is what the depth of philosophy is.)

I am definitely confused, but equally intrigued, by paragraph §111. First, I can’t figure out if Wittgenstein is saying the problems that arise through a “misinterpretation of our forms of language” are actually deep or they just have the “characteristics of depth.” He explains they are “as deeply rooted in us as the forms of our language.” On one hand, this grounds the “depth” within the person. But while this depth initially seemed physically tangible to me, evoking an image of a physical tree of knowledge/forms/misunderstandings “rooted” in my body, there is no true physicality to depth within ourselves. The depth of forms of language is unclear. I think part of untangling this requires divorcing the philosophical associations with depth from his reference to depth here. In tandem with philosophy, I see depth as complicated, abstract, and heavier than reality. Wittgenstein condemns this philosophical depth that estranges philosophy from reality in §87, §97, and §109. In §111, the term depth seems to refer to a level of internal entanglement that shapes our perspectives, understanding, and consciousness. While this isn’t physically tangible, its tangibility springs from the feeling of a “grammatical joke to be deep.” I think this feeling serves as a base for the depth he is referring to. He aligns the depth of philosophy with the depth of grammatical jokes on this base. I still can’t fully wrap my head around his claim: whether the “disquietudes’” characteristics of depth are rooted in the feeling of the joke or estranged from that feeling. I also can’t tell if a philosopher would take offense to this, if this measure of depth could be seen as more superficial than the philosophical, abstract sense of depth I described before. I also don’t know if Wittgenstein is trying to undermine the hierarchies of “depth” within feeling, language, and philosophy.

Nevertheless, Wittgenstein’s more grounded view of philosophy resonates strongly with me. I have struggled to naturally connect what I learn in philosophy classes with my everyday life. I used to wonder if I should always be thinking “deeply,” juggling different philosophical frameworks at all times. But what I take from Wittgenstein so far is that philosophy should not be an artificial burden that complicates my experience of life. Instead, it should help me “clear away the fog” created by those “deep disquietudes,” so that I can see what is natural and clear. This way of thinking about philosophy excites me.
