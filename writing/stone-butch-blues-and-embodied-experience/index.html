Stone Butch Blues and Embodied Experience

April 6, 2025

Written for Existentialism at Duke, Spring 2025.

Jess experiences traumatic encounters with men in her life, who take every opportunity to rough her and the others like her up. In the beginning, after a violent encounter with the cops, Al receded into the bathroom in silence, drawing a bath. This is a ritual Jess invoked when she got assaulted by the football players. She did it again after the group of cops brutally assaulted her, but her bathtub turned pink from the “current of red water” (67) between her legs. I think the repeated act of taking a bath speaks to a larger truth about attempting to reclaim the body and embodied experience after it feels out of the person’s control. The bathtub is a place where the physicality of the body is centered and relaxed. Your muscles can ease, and in that ease, the mind can resync with the body. It speaks to the extreme trauma Jess experienced that her bath was marred with red blood. It also speaks to the strength of Jess and Al to attempt to get more in touch with their physicality instead of ignoring it. While hopefully not as extreme and traumatic as Jess’s experience, people often feel a loss of control over their body: when you are in pain but must ignore it to get on with the day, when your body feels especially vulnerable to external judgement, when you feel nauseous or sick because you are overwhelmed. There is an instinct to maintain that alienation through actions that are harmful to the self. It takes extra control and self-love to put in the work to reconnect with your self and body. For me, an important practice is meditation, which allows me to get back in touch with my body and aware of my embodied existence. The increased awareness brings a greater sense of peace and acceptance towards the fluctuations of power and powerlessness, health and sickness, that living through a body naturally brings. I understood the ritual of the bath as a practice which serves the same purpose.
