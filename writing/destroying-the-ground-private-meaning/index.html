Destroying the Ground / Private Meaning

September 21, 2025

Written for Wittgenstein and Literary Theory at Duke, Fall 2025.

I hope that the confusion I’ve felt during this week’s readings means I am in the process of shedding the theoretical pictures that Wittgenstein argues against. The biggest shift for me came from Chapter 3 of Toril Moi’s Revolution of the Ordinary, which worked to undo my desire for an ideal theory of language. I especially connected with this quote: “Wittgenstein is trying to get us to see that any talk about the grounds of language—about some general features (a theory) that secures meaning as such, or simply the meaning a particular language-game—is nonsensical” (Moi, 82). This was the first time I actually understood that the answers I had been looking for at the start of this class, exactly how language functions through a complete, unifying theory, would not be provided due to Wittgenstein’s view of the nature of language. I now see the force of his claim that a universal theory of language is an example of “language on vacation,” because of its abstraction and estrangement from actual use. Instead, what Wittgenstein offers is a way of “clearing up regions” of confusion in our language. However, while I understand this logically, I still feel unstable in my grasp of language theory because the “ground” has literally been destroyed from below me. This week, I am working to get comfortable with this shift, and I’m curious to see what “regions” will be clarified, and how that will affect the way I think about and use language.

Another point of interest this week for me was the concept of public/private in language. While I didn’t pay much attention to §95 last week, upon rereading it, it has become one of my new favorites. It answers a question I was struggling with in the first week, which is where to put private, psychological meanings.

§95: “‘Thinking must be something unique.’ When we say, mean, that such-and-such is the case, then, with what we mean, we do not stop anywhere short of the fact, but mean: such-and-such — is — thus-and-so.— But this paradox (which indeed has the form of a truism) can also be expressed in this way: one can think what is not the case” (Wittgenstein, 48–49).

It took me some time to untangle this paragraph. First, Wittgenstein points to a common temptation I personally fall into: to treat thinking as a private act of representing reality, as if meaning originates in an inner mental space. I assume that because a thought is in my head, it must be unique, true, or guaranteed in its meaning. But when I think something that isn’t the case, due to imagination or bias or a variety of factors, I take an event or object to mean something it is not. If I assume that my private thought connects directly to the fact itself, a paradox emerges: how can I ever be mistaken if my meaning already “does not stop anywhere short of the fact”? Therefore, meaning does not arise from an internal, private act of thinking secured by a fact in public reality; instead, it must be located in the public language games, where there is room to mean something and be right or wrong about it according to public rules.
