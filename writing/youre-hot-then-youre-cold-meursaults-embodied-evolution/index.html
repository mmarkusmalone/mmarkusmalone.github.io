You’re Hot Then You’re Cold: Meursault’s Embodied Evolution

March 30, 2025

Written for Existentialism at Duke, Spring 2025.

The Stranger by Albert Camus follows an apathetic narrator, Meursault, who comes to consciousness when he murders an unnamed Arab in colonial Algeria. It is split into two parts. In the first part, the main character and narrator Meursault describes his daily existence, where he lives moment-to-moment, through his physicality. In the second half, he is put on trial, where he reflects for the first time. Camus’s work springs from his absurdist philosophy. In absurdism, existence has no inherent meaning. In this meaninglessness rises the absurd, the paradox of humans continuing to try and find meaning, asking for answers, even with the impossibility of the world answering adequately. To come to terms with the absurd means learning to bear the silence of the world when we ask it for answers. What follows this is a freedom in acknowledging our limits, happiness in living in the present with no promised future, a booming self in the silence of the world. When Meursault develops existential consciousness, his relationship with the physical world evolves; this evolution enables him to accept the absurd, where he finds true happiness and freedom.

Meursault lives sensuously in the moment; still, his sensitivity to the physical forces in the world overwhelms him, exposing a lack of freedom in his unconsciousness. At the end of part one, his sensual experience of the beach transforms from an idyllic fantasy into an overwhelming hellscape, where he gets into a confrontation with the Arab. Meursault struggles to orient himself in the heat:

It was this burning, which I couldn’t stand anymore, that made me move forward. I knew that it was stupid, that I wouldn’t get the sun off me by stepping forward. But I took a step, one step, forward. … At the same instant the sweat in my eyebrows dripped down over my eyelids all at once and covered them with a warm, thick film. My eyes were blinded behind the curtain of tears and salt. All I could feel were the cymbals of sunlight crashing on my forehead and, indistinctly, the dazzling spear flying up from the knife in front of me (Camus, 59).

The “burning” is the active subject of the first sentence, who “made” Meursault move forward. This exposes Meursault’s weakness to his senses. He is desperate to shake off the heat, the burning, as it compels him to take a step forward. Even in the moment, he knows the futility of the act because it “wouldn’t get the sun off me.” The heat works further to hurt him with his own sweat, which he describes as a “warm, thick film” and “curtain of tears and salt.” His body’s reaction to the heat, perspiration and tears, is what blinds him. His body is his own prison, trapping him in his physicality and blinding him to his agency. Still, comparing the blindness to a “curtain” leaves open the possibility of it being drawn, of Meursault coming to existential consciousness. Camus builds on the concrete powers of the sun, its heat and blinding light, with the abstract, sweat as a “curtain” and “crashing cymbals” that stun Meursault. The “cymbals of sunlight” emphasizes the disorientation that Meursault feels in the moment, the complete break in his previous harmony with nature while swimming. In the absurdist framework, the world is silent and unforgiving. Meursault lives through his senses, so his experience is controlled by this vicious world. He is unfree due to his absolute sensuality that estranges him from his internality, which he could use to exert power over his senses. He can exist with a sense of happiness when the temperature is nice, but when something as simple as the heat overwhelms him, his entire being is paralyzed. Yet, it is in his complete discordance with the physical world here where Meursault can assert himself for the first time.

When the gun fires, Meursault frees himself from the disorientation of being controlled by his unforgiving senses. The Arab confronted him with a knife, while he gripped the gun under his jacket:

It seemed to me as if the sky split open from one end to the other to rain down fire. My whole being tensed and I squeezed my hand around the revolver. The trigger gave; I felt the smooth underside of the butt; and there, in that noise, sharp and deafening at the same time, is where it all started. I shook off the sweat and sun. I knew that I had shattered the harmony of the day, the exceptional silence of a beach where I’d been happy (Camus, 59).

Following from the power of the sun transcending to the abstract, deafening Meursault, Camus further mythologizes nature when the sky splits open “from one end to the other to rain down fire.” In the Old Testament, “rain fire and brimstone” is used to describe God’s judgment and destruction, as seen in the story of Sodom and Gomorrah (“Fire and Brimstone”). Camus uses it here to represent the destruction of Meursault’s unconscious relationship with the physical world. Meursault “tenses” in response to this. The tension represents his absolute paralysis, internal defenselessness to his sensuality. But it is in this complete tension that he squeezes his hand, the first act he establishes for himself in the passage with an active “I squeezed,” and shoots the gun. He does not have consciousness yet, does not understand what he is doing, but he acts outside of the force of nature. He grounds himself in the feeling of the “smooth underside of the butt” of the gun. The sensation of “smooth” contrasts the “burning,” “stinging,” “scorching,” and “crashing” he felt before. His disorientation, complete helplessness to the intense sensuality, falls away. When the gun fires, the noise is “sharp” and “deafening.” The sense of definition in the “sharpness” of the firing gun contrasts the echo and disorder of the “crashing cymbal” he felt before. This feeling of clarity, Meursault asserts, “is where it all started.” He orients himself with nature, shaking off the sweat that blinded him and the sun that burned him. With this new orientation he shatters the “harmony of the day” and “exceptional silence” of the beach. For the first time, he is aware of the happiness he had in the moments he was in “harmony” with the physical world. He is also aware of the “exceptional silence” he was living in as an unconscious being. The silence is forever gone with this new understanding. But he is united with his internal, an existential consciousness that leads to self-realization.

In part two, Meursault is reoriented in his relationship with the physical world, and further, his consciousness, in the absurd world. Meursault waits out the final days in prison before his execution. The chaplain begged him to find God, which he angrily defied. He calms down when the chaplain leaves. He writes:

I must have fallen asleep, because I woke up with the stars in my face. Sounds of the countryside were drifting in. Smells of night, earth, and salt air were cooling my temples. The wondrous peace of that sleeping summer flowed through me like a tide (Camus, 122).

Meursault is in a prison cell with only one small window, yet he wakes up with the “stars in his face.” This highlights Meursault’s unmarred power to live vividly and connected to nature despite his confines. Meursault still experiences the moment sensually, aware of the “sounds of the countryside” and “smells of night, earth, and salt air.” Instead of assaulting and disorienting him, it was “cooling his temples,” which evokes an image of clarity. With his new consciousness, he internalizes his embodied experiences with lucidity. He can live in the peaceful moment, allowing it to “flow” through him like a “tide,” while maintaining a keen awareness of his condition and self. It does not crash over him like the tide at the beach. This shows Meursault’s ability to reconcile with the absurd by living with the world, an idea developed further when he refers back to his confrontation with the chaplain:

As if that blind rage had washed me clean, rid me of hope; for the first time, in that night alive with signs and stars, I opened myself to the gentle indifference of the world. Finding it so much like myself—so like a brother, really—I felt that I had been happy and that I was happy again (Camus, 122).

The imagery of water is evoked again, where he is “washed clean” by being rid of hope. In an absurd world, to live in truth means to give up false notions of hope for an answer to “meaning.” It is a reversal of a baptism, where the chaplain was able to cleanse him from any hope for a savior or meaning of it all. It also heals the destruction of his relationship with the physical world in the “rain of fire” at the beach, as he accepts the meaninglessness of life, the “indifference of the world.” This does not provoke a crisis for Meursault; instead, it provides him peace and freedom. This is what he ends up identifying with: the physical world is like a “brother.” His disconnect with abstract meanings others hold onto is healed by his likeness to the physical world, which holds the same “indifference.” He finds freedom and peace in this balance of reflection and living sensuously. In this freedom he “felt that [he] had been happy and that [he] was happy again.” There is happiness in letting the moment “flow” through, feeling fully alive and truly free in oneself while accepting one’s limits. Meursault reconciles with the absurd, allowing him to triumph over it.

Meursault represents an absurdist ideal: fully conscious of his dire fate and the meaninglessness of it, yet living happily and freely in his embodied experience. He does not rush his death or wish it away. Most people in the world fall into either side (I wish it away, please!). We bury the inevitability of death under pressing assignments or promises of meaning. But the absurd asks us to look square at our life and our finitude and try not to make it pretty with any hope or meaning. If one can do this, they can live in authenticity and truth, with the power of life exuding from the simple act of living and trying. It exudes from the sensuous experience of each moment, from the acceptance of our frailty, and understanding that we will still ask what the “meaning of life is” occasionally, that the answer will never come.
