Hedda Gabler

February 15, 2025

Written for Existentialism at Duke, Spring 2025.

I noticed the dynamic of muse and artist from The Seducer’s Diary come up again in Ibsen’s Hedda Gabler. In The Seducer’s Diary, from the perspective of the aesthetic narrator, a way for a girl to become “interesting” is through experience with a man. Johannes works as a self-proclaimed artist, shaping Cordelia’s character and emotions like a sculpture. Similarly, Mrs. Elvsted serves as a muse for Löveborg. In conversation with Hedda Gabler, Mrs. Elvsted says, “And he.. for his part… he’s made me into sort of a real person. Taught me to think … and to understand quite a lot of things” (Ibsen, 190). Mrs. Elvsted was awakened through her romance with Löveborg. She was also an inspiration for his masterpiece, just as Cordelia was for Johannes. The power dynamics in the passive “muse” figures being shaped by the more active male characters are more complicated in Hedda Gabler, though. Mrs. Elvsted says, “I got sort of a control over him” (Ibsen, 190). Mrs. Elvsted is not simply a passive muse: she collaborates with Löveborg on his book and is given credit for it. She also exercises her agency by running away from her unhappy marriage, an act Hedda Gabler won’t even do. Still, despite her claim of control over Löveborg, she is reduced to a witness and victim of circumstance as Hedda provokes him to spiral. Even in her courageous and “awakened” state, she is still victim to the whims of another man, which shows the limit of her agency. I am curious as to what this complication of the “muse” figure in Hedda Gabler is meant to reveal next to the character of Hedda and her own struggle with agency.
