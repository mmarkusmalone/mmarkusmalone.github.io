I am Possible: Authenticity as Movement

April 2025

Written for Existentialism at Duke, Spring 2025.

The existential tradition empowers the individual to define their life for themselves. Kierkegaard defines this process as “virkeliggørelse,” or self-realization, where an individual becomes fully conscious and lives authentically. For Kierkegaard, an individual leads an authentic life by accepting who they are and taking responsibility for their actions. Kierkegaard’s philosophy rejects the answers in the objective and embraces subjectivity. Nietzsche also rejects objectivity, going further to define man and rules as having no meaning in themselves. What rises from this is the power of the individual to define their own life. Nietzsche’s bird of prey is an ideal of authenticity—resisting conformity to societal norms, going forward full speed through life, not held back by guilt or conformity. As existential thinkers turn to the ideal of authenticity as a key for self-realization, it is important to explore what authenticity actually means—its qualities, possibilities, and limits. Sartre develops the idea of a structurally free and dynamic consciousness that resists identity and definition. If the conscious subject is to be authentic, then authenticity should exist in the dynamism of existence. Fanon and Beauvoir contend with the tensions between this freedom in consciousness that gives rise to authenticity and the inflexibility inherent to the situation of being “othered.” Beauvoir specifically focuses on “lived experience,” which “designates the whole of a person’s subjectivity. More particularly the term describes the way an individual makes sense of her situation and actions” (Moi). Lived experience enfolds the gaze of the other without reducing the subject to it. Its totality dynamically responds to the subject’s situation and “otherness.” While authenticity’s roots in the present are complicated in its coupling with the element of lived experience, the spontaneous persists. In tracing the concept of authenticity through these thinkers, it becomes a non-reductive construction of the subject, embedded in our evolving lived experience and fueled by difference and spontaneity.

In Nausea, Sartre abandons his historical biography project of Marquis de Rollebon, highlighting the impossibility in an authentic reconstruction of a subject. Initially, the project of writing the biography is what justifies Antoine Roquentin’s life. He slowly becomes disillusioned with his project and justification through inconsistencies in Marquis de Rollebon’s letters. Roquentin writes:

You couldn’t dream of a better life than his: but did he live it? If only his letters weren’t so formal … Ah, I wish I had known his look, perhaps he had a charming way of leaning his head on his shoulder or mischievously placing his long index on his nose, or sometimes, between two polished lies, having a sudden fit of violence which he stifles immediately (Sartre, 58).

Life is a noun, a static reference to the totality of one’s experiences. When Sartre asks, “did he live it?” he questions the subjective, dynamic experience of Marquis de Rollebon within his situation. By italicizing “If only his letters weren’t so formal,” Sartre sarcastically draws attention to the futility of Roquentin’s reconstruction. The formality of letters is not the issue in capturing de Rollebon’s subjective experience; rather, the entire concept of an authentic reconstruction of the past is flawed. The “charming” or “mischievous” intangible aspects of de Rollebon can only be felt in his movements, in existence. Further, the “sudden” is also rooted in the present; one cannot feel the surprise and urgency of suddenness in retrospect. The intangible and spontaneous are key parts of one’s humanity, and they are constantly developing through one’s experiences with the external. In his abandonment of his project, he rejects the quest for constructing authenticity in the restoration of a static individual as a justification for life. It marks a shift from the past to the present, static to dynamic, in the search for self-realization. With the abandonment of the past, Roquentin finds freedom and possibility in the present, in his existence.

Sartre further develops the idea of the dynamic self through the disempowerment of the past referential “I,” and empowerment of existence. Towards the end of the novel, Roquentin sheds the desire for a justification for his life. His clarity extends to his perception of self:

Now when I say “I,” it seems hollow to me. I can’t manage to feel myself very well, I am so forgotten. The only real thing left in me is existence which feels it exists. I yawn, lengthily. No one. Antoine Roquentin exists for no one. That amuses me. And just what is Antoine Roquentin? An abstraction. A pale reflection of myself wavers in my consciousness. Antoine Roquentin … and suddenly the “I” pales, pales, and fades out (Sartre, 170).

Sartre first draws the distinction between the “I” that speaks and the “hollow” “I” that the speaker is referring to. The “hollow” quality of the past referential to the self refers to its reductive nature. It does not allow Roquentin to “feel” himself because of what is forgotten, reduced, in the past referential. The reduction inherent in this “I” mirrors the reduction in Roquentin’s historical reconstruction of Marquis de Rollebon. The human inability to coincide oneself with a declarative “I” indicates that there is movement in the meantime. In this movement, one is transcending themselves. This movement is contained in “existence” which “feels” itself. In feeling his existence, the past referential of identity symbolized by his name, “Antoine Roquentin,” is diluted to a “pale reflection” that “fades out.” There is structural freedom embedded in consciousness—it never coincides with itself and thus resists any attempts to essentialize it. This freedom lends itself to a dynamic construction of lived experience rooted in existence. The “yawn” that follows is an example of this. The yawn is spontaneous, unthinking yet whole in its action and fulfillment. It is a suspension of the self where the subject is literally freely opening itself to air and engagement with the world. The body takes the fruits of this and integrates it with the subject. What follows is that authenticity lies in this continued movement and construction rather than in the reconstruction of a hollow self.

In Black Orpheus, Sartre contradicts his resistance to “hollowness” by essentializing the black consciousness and denying black people Roquentin’s freedom and movement in existence. Sartre argues that existence precedes essence, meaning our choices and actions define who we are, rather than being pre-determined by some inherent nature. Still, he essentializes the black consciousness. In Chapter 5 of Black Skin, White Masks, Fanon criticizes Sartre’s denial of the black subjective in Black Orpheus. Fanon highlights the consequences of Sartre’s essentialization:

In terms of consciousness, black consciousness claims to be an absolute density, full of itself, a stage pre-existent to any opening, to any abolition of the self by desire (Fanon, 113).

An “absolute” and dense consciousness opposes the porous and contingent. It denies the black subject any possibility of difference and change, which authenticity springs from. In being “pre-existent,” Fanon situates the root of the essentialization of the black consciousness in the past, perceived to be sealed from any attempts of the present black subject. While Fanon is highlighting the misrepresentation of the black consciousness, he is exposing the true fixedness that the “gaze of the other” locks the black consciousness in. The situation of the black subject, created by this gaze, restrains the movements of the “free consciousness.” This complicates the becoming of the black subject in the pursuit of non-reductive authenticity.

The movement of the black subject responds to its perceived essentialization, limiting the freedom of their movements. Fanon highlights how the lived experience of the black subject can be deeply rigid. He writes:

I am overdetermined from the outside. I am a slave not to the “idea” others have of me, but to my appearance. I arrive slowly in the world; sudden emergences are no longer my habit. I crawl along. The white gaze, the only valid one, is already dissecting me. I am fixed (Fanon, 95).

Fanon first roots the pervasiveness of being “overdetermined” not in the abstract landscape of “idea” but in physical existence as his “appearance.” A level of spontaneity is denied to the black man. When Fanon writes, “sudden emergences are no longer my habit,” I read it as a reference to the social rigidity that educates the black man’s movements. Crafted by the essentialization of black men as a threat, the black man has learned not to make sudden, spontaneous movements. He must “arrive slowly” to negotiate his physicality with the situation he is in. This rigidity is burdening; it reduces his movements to a “crawl.” In this weak and vulnerable state, the “white gaze” further “dissects him.” Dissection reduces the object of dissection to an immobile state. The object is vulnerable and open; the boundaries between him and the “white gaze” break down as the gaze has access to all his organs, muscles, thoughts. This creates a state of “fixed” for Fanon, and further, the black subject “dissected” by the white gaze. The fixedness that is deeply embedded in the existence of the black man opposes the freedom Roquentin finds in existence. Roquentin is able to forget himself, let his name pale away in the fullness of his existence, but black men are not allowed that complete freedom if they are to survive under the watch of the white gaze.

The fixedness is mirrored in the situation of women in the patriarchal society, as put forth by Beauvoir. In The Second Sex, Beauvoir explains how the myth of the woman works to undermine the female subject’s freedom. Her situation is also inflexible:

The girl will be wife, mother, grandmother; she will take care of her house exactly as her mother does, she will take care of her children as she was taken care of: she is twelve years old, and her story is already written in the heavens; she will discover it day after day without shaping it; she is curious but frightened when she thinks about this life whose very step is planned in advance and toward which each day irrevocably moves her (Beauvoir, 360).

First, Beauvoir situates the roles of a woman in relation to the husband, child, and grandchild. Beauvoir shows how the woman’s autonomy in her self-creation is undermined when her roles exist in service to others. Beauvoir also employs parallelism in the sentences that begin with “she will take care of …” to highlight the determinability of the female’s becoming, passed down by generations of women before. The girl is put in a passive role. She is “discovering,” “curious,” and “frightened” by this inflexibility but not actively responding to it. Instead, “life” is an active subject, eerily “advancing” towards her. This exhibits the power that the situation has over the girl. The passive movement of the girl mars the notion of pure freedom in her existence.

One could argue that if the movements of the black and female subjects are more rigid, then their dynamic constructions of lived experience are less authentic. This argument reduces the subject to the inflexibility of their situation. Authenticity is situated not in the subject’s situation but in the movement of the subject as it exists. In the movement of the subject, there is difference. If there wasn’t difference, then there would not be a lack of cohesion between the conscious “I” and its past referential. The capacity for difference is not reduced for the black and female subjects. While the black subject may be limited in their physical spontaneity, they are not limited in a spontaneous reaction to their limited physical spontaneity. Fanon writes, “Jean-Paul Sartre forgets that the black man suffers in his body quite differently from the white man” (Fanon, 117). The difference in the black man’s “suffering” proves this. Suffering is a dynamic response to the rigidity of the situation. The same holds for female subjects: they can spontaneously respond to their difference to the myths that surround them. In the acceptance and harnessing of the differences embedded in one’s lived experience, the subject can engage in authentic construction.

Fanon suffers in the inflexibility of his situation; his response to total “fixedness” demonstrates the possibility of black authenticity even in dire circumstances. At the end of Chapter 5, Fanon quotes a cripple who told his brother to accept his blackness as he did his amputation. Fanon rejects this advice and writes:

When I opened my eyes yesterday I saw the sky in total revulsion. I tried to get up but the eviscerated silence surged toward me with paralyzed wings. Not responsible for my acts, at the crossroads between Nothingness and Infinity, I began to weep (Fanon, 119).

The image of opening eyes has connotations of clarity, and further, having a transparent awareness of reality. For the black man, this clarity involves being aware of the pervasiveness of the white gaze. The sky is a symbol of vastness, infinity. In having infinity “revulsed” by the black subject, Fanon underscores the inescapability of the black man’s situation. By trying to get up, Fanon tries to fight against his situation. But the “eviscerated silence surged toward” him and opposed his movement. He “eviscerated” the silence and gave it “paralyzed wings.” Throughout the chapter, Fanon uses imagery of dissection and disability to describe the black subject under the white gaze. The “silence” is not a full, empowering act; instead, it is a mangled and lousy response to his situation. This situates him in a paradoxical situation, where both inaction and action recede from him. Situated here, Fanon “began to weep.” With tears, Fanon responds to his “fixed” state. Weeping is an expression of suffering. It indicates depth of grief in the black subject that resists the flattening white gaze. In weeping, Fanon asserts his subjectivity. Fanon embraces the possibility of human spontaneity. Even in total situated fixedness, there persists the dynamic freedom in consciousness. Arising from this spontaneity, the subject can engage in authentic construction that colors lived experience with subjectivity and differentness.

Beauvoir also embraces the possibility of authenticity for women, emphasizing its relation to lived experience. Beauvoir outlines the situation of the adolescent girl. In the age of becoming and growing excitement, the girl’s eyes are also opened to the lack of autonomy offered to women in the modern world. Still, Beauvoir maintains the girl’s opportunity to be free:

However, this situation that the girl flees by a thousand inauthentic paths is also one that she sometimes assumes authentically. Her shortcomings make her irritating: but her unique virtues sometimes make her astonishing. Both have the same origin. From her rejection of the world, from her unsettled waiting, and from her nothingness, she can create a springboard for herself and emerge then in her solitude and freedom (Beauvoir, 429).

The act of “fleeing” seems to be a natural reaction to the fixedness of the girl’s situation; it seems like an act of authentic construction. Still, Beauvoir writes that the girl will flee by “inauthentic” paths. This highlights a nuance within authenticity. Not every active choice and subjective response to a situation is an authentic act. “Fleeing” one’s situation is inauthentic because it means fleeing from a truth of existence, an aspect of one’s lived experience. But the buildup of a “thousand” of these inauthentic paths opens the possibility of the girl “assuming” her situation “authentically.” Even in a rejection of the situation, the girl will inadvertently craft her own subjectivity in the accumulation of her lived experiences and differences. In this way, the girl’s authentic construction exists in the differences in the evolution of many movements, rather than in an act of spontaneity rooted in existence. In this evolution, the girl will come to “assume” her situation, and therefore lived experience, as her own. This backwards acceptance creates a foundation for intentional and dynamic authentic construction. Interestingly, “Whether or not the black man can recover a fully authentic subjectivity, according to Fanon’s philosophy, is uncertain” (Hiddleston). Beauvoir leaves this possibility open for the girl in her assumption of her situation. Still, for both, spontaneous authentic construction, like weeping, is still possible for a subject that has yet to recover a fully authentic subjectivity. With this, authenticity is evolving and dynamic, but also already existent in lived experience. When one cannot “forget themselves,” a non-reductive authenticity is still constructed in the fullness of existence, but intentional acts are educated by subjectivity that is crafted through lived experience. As a result of this, the girl becomes “irritating,” yet “astonishing,” in her “unique virtues,” which are the differences in the girl’s subjectivity. With a foundation of authenticity in one’s lived experience, she can “emerge” and exist in the dynamic and free act of authentic construction.

With this notion of authenticity, realizing oneself becomes realizing the inconsistencies and possibilities of the self. One does not have to be bogged down by the idea of “staying true” to a reductive notion of self and situation that doesn’t serve oneself in the present. The “true self” arises from acceptance of one’s situation rather than devotion or rejection of it (and it will no longer be “true” by the time you can congratulate yourself for finding it). By embracing one’s dynamism through a freer engagement with the world, one can craft a life that is similarly exciting and authentic.
