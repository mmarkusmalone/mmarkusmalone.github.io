Responsibility

January 21, 2026

What I find especially interesting about these articles on responsibility is the concept of moral entanglement, especially as it relates to humans and AI systems. It provided me with a new framework to think about the responsibility designers have when creating these technologies. If a technology has the power to disrupt existing systems and social structures, then it should be designed with an awareness of and responsibility for that ability. I think that it is a fault in the tech industry culture, and computer science education, where technologists are motivated to simply maximize efficiency, reachability, and their own ego rather than responsibly maximizing the societal impact of their technology, especially when that technology is powerful enough to create a moral entanglement with users.

From my reply to a classmate, January 23, 2026:

The idea you brought up of blame in this case is interesting. I feel like it would be most natural for engineers to avoid blame, and all of the negative feelings that come with it, when something goes wrong in the system. And with different corporate layers, decision-makers, and contributing technologies, it seems extremely easy for any blame-avoidant engineer to shift it to a different factor in the accident. But since blame works to help the person experiencing it avoid the mistake again, the lack of it could prevent an engineer from truly learning and taking the necessary safety steps in their next design. I think that there should be frameworks in place such that blame is fairly distributed, so that those mistakes deeply educate future designs of autonomous systems.

Safety: Access

January 25, 2026

In the material, I noticed that access is a large factor behind the existential risk AI poses to humans. If a superintelligent system has access to networks to mobilize other systems, weapons to hurt humans, and logins to control human accounts, that is where real-world harm can be done.

In the AI ecosystem today, there is a booming “agent” economy where LLMs have access to database APIs, user operating systems, and websites to independently complete tasks for users. Already, there are stories of AI agents deleting entire company databases, making surprising changes to user accounts, and many other similar mistakes. In my own experience designing an autonomous AI agent system, most of my time was spent with the head of security going over correct permissions for the agent and what tools we felt secure giving it access to. I think that companies should bear similar weight to prevent AI systems from harming uneducated users now. And in the future, I think that making extremely cautious decisions about what external tools AI systems have access to is paramount. Beyond making sure the user grants explicit permissions, the intelligent agent must be aligned to not trick the user into granting permissions unknowingly, or override a user input to access it independently. These alignment problems within access seem crucial to avoiding disaster. I am scared for when this prevention gets in the way of profitability in the agent economy, and what harm smarter AI with more access can cause in the future.

Safety: Deskilling

January 28, 2026

What I found interesting from the readings was the idea of “deskilling” as not just a societal harm, but a safety risk of AI. The example of a pilot not being able to perform an emergency landing as well if they had been practicing with autonomous systems is terrifying. As professionals become more and more reliant on AI to perform their specialization, what will the actual impacts be on society? There are also examples from middle and elementary schools where kids are less capable of basic writing and grammar skills because they use ChatGPT for their schoolwork. While this is worrying, it is still a question of how this will truly hurt their futures and experiences in the real world. I think writing is an important skill, but people who don’t can argue that using a tool to write everything will be the norm, and writing can become an outdated skill just as churning butter or operating a fax machine is today.
