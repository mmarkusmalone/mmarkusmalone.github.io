The Aesthete’s Manipulation: An Interplay of Control, Façade, and Truth

February 23, 2025

Written for Existentialism at Duke, Spring 2025.

Kierkegaard defines three stages for post-awakening: the aesthetic, the ethical, and the religious. Two characters, Johannes the Seducer from Kierkegaard’s The Seducer’s Diary and Hedda Gabler from Ibsen’s Hedda Gabler, exist in the aesthetic stage. Johannes the Seducer was written to embody the aesthete. Even if she was not written to fit into Kierkegaard’s aesthetic stage, Hedda Gabler is a clear aesthete, as her values, the curation of beauty and the “interesting,” align with the aesthetic ideals. The aesthete’s concern with the external world—beauty, performance, and the interesting—stunts the development of an inner authenticity, the key to living in truth for Kierkegaard. Further, the aesthete’s yearning for the “interesting” and “theatrical” obliges them to aestheticize the world, warping their perception of the external and internal. Aestheticization flattens the multidimensional external by reducing people to one-dimensional characters they can use in their pursuit of beauty. Additionally, the blanket of beauty is thrown over the random, the meaningless, the complexities within the external, further masking the real. This is the flattened world that the aesthete lives in and acts through, the aesthetic dimension, which I will refer to in this essay. The aesthete uses the act of manipulation to control the external, and the façade of control that this act reinforces within the aesthetic dimension estranges them from existence in the truth.

Johannes the Seducer uses manipulation of women to reinforce his sense of control, yet this control collapses inward, reducing his external “control” to a mere illusion within the aesthetic dimension. On his internality amid his burgeoning love for Cordelia, Johannes writes:

“I scarcely know myself. My mind roars like a turbulent sea in the storms of passion. If someone else could see my soul in this state, it would seem to him that it, like a skiff, plunged prow-first down into the ocean, as if in its dreadful momentum it would have to steer down into the depths of the abyss. He does not see that high on the mast a sailor is on the lookout” (Kierkegaard, 33).

First, the contradictions that exist within the construction of the scene are the first hint to the lack of control Johannes has over “passion.” One cannot logically locate Johannes himself within the metaphors of waves, boat, and sailor. Also, Johannes inserts a third-person observer, “he,” into his internal reflection. This set of eyes sets the stage for Johannes to perform inside of himself. He sets the scene of the drama by aligning his mind with an uncontrollable force and his soul with a small boat that can only be rocked by this force. But he undermines this image of powerlessness with a portrayal of the heroic: the “sailor on the lookout,” one in control of the turbulent seas within himself. Still, the sailor remains hidden from the observer of his mind. He may do this because the sailor, or otherwise his control, does not exist. Another interpretation is that an observer can see the sailor’s true lack of command over the sea. Johannes’ theater, his internal manipulation to convince himself of his control, works to aestheticize his internal. Through this self-aestheticization and flattening, he attempts to assert his self-mastery, estranging himself from the truth of his internality. This façade of control over his internality informs his external. Cordelia exists as the central muse in his aesthetic external, so his successful project of seducing her reinforces his idea of control over the external. But after his project, “A” writes that Cordelia “does not see the criminal in him, but neither does she see the noble person—she feels him only esthetically” (Kierkegaard, 12). Even though Johannes believes in the success of his manipulation, he could never successfully bestow on Cordelia a level of “depth” when his external exists on the flattened dimension of the aesthetic plane. He only harnesses the powers of the senses—erotic love, excitement, rejection—so his control can only be felt “esthetically.” But for Johannes, his control over the external is total because his external exists in the aesthetic dimension. The discrepancy between external reality, which cannot be controlled, and Johannes’ version of the external, which he exerts total control over, further alienates him from the truth that exists in the external and internal. His alienation mirrors Hedda Gabler’s, which drives her to manipulate and control as well.

Hedda Gabler’s attempted manipulation of beauty outside of herself works to estrange her from the realities of the external. Thea’s hair is described as full, blonde, and wavy, which contrasts Hedda’s brown and thinner hair. When they meet, Thea recounts how she was terrified of Hedda in school because she would pull her hair and threaten “to burn it off” (Ibsen, 186). This juvenile bullying exposes Hedda’s deep-rooted insecurity around her own beauty. Hedda’s insecurity and jealousy coincide with a desire to control the beauty of the external—if Hedda can control beauty in the external, she has beauty. This contrived ideal of control exists within the aesthetic dimension because Hedda reduces Thea to her beauty, and even further, her beauty into a power that can be controlled and possessed. One could argue that she does succeed in her attempt to control by frightening Thea. But, by frightening Thea, she is reinforcing her own ugliness and Thea’s purity when her goal is to achieve beauty. Further, she can exert control over Thea only esthetically, through fear, as Johannes does over Cordelia. This façade of control does not have the power to fulfill her true desire. Her bullying also establishes Hedda’s deep desire to burn Thea’s hair. Burning is an act to disintegrate, to destroy. It is within Hedda’s power to destroy what she cannot control. She uses destruction in her manipulation of Lovborg and Thea in the act of burning the manuscript. She says to herself, “Now I’m burning your child, Thea! With your curly hair! Your child and Ejlert Lovborg’s. I’m burning… burning your child” (Ibsen, 246). In this moment, Hedda exists within the aesthetic dimension. She aestheticizes the manuscript, bestowing it with meaning beyond itself: Thea’s beauty, Lovborg’s love, both their fates. In this aestheticization, she crafts her façade of control over all these external forces and disconnects herself from reality. This destruction, even with the drama and beauty Hedda injects into it, does not give her the power to control the external. Yet, in her ecstasy, she exists in the aesthetic dimension where she does have this power. This creates an estrangement that renders her manipulation futile. Lovborg dies in a manner antithetical to the beautiful goal of her manipulation. Thea works with Tessman in the end to recreate the manuscript, undermining her act of destruction. The failure of Hedda’s manipulations stems from the fact that they are constructed and executed within her flattened aesthetic dimension, estranged from the truth of the external.

The characters of Hedda and Johannes diverge in relation to their class and gender. These differences make them very different characters with different contexts that educate the ends to which they manipulate. In the interest of space, I will focus on their commonalities as embodiments of the aesthete. The aesthete’s project of manipulation serves to reinforce their delusion of power and control: Johannes believes he can completely control Cordelia because he was able to seduce her; Hedda believes she can manipulate Lovborg’s death and control Thea’s beauty because she can destroy. But their manipulations do not prove fruitful: Johannes was only able to control Cordelia esthetically, Lovborg’s death was ugly, and Thea’s beauty outlived Hedda. The futility is inherent in the aesthete’s manipulation because their control only exists in the flattened, aestheticized dimension; the end to which a manipulator desires total control over the reality cannot be achieved when they are deeply estranged from that truth. The irony is that the aesthete lives in a fragile dimension, where they are blind to their own estrangement from reality. Johannes can continue living because he remains blind. However, the destruction of the aesthetic dimension in which Hedda took in the world, where she was in control of the external, meant the destruction of her life.

The desire to manipulate and control goes hand-in-hand with the inability to cope with the overwhelming disorder in our modern lives. When someone attempts to manipulate someone else, they are looking for security and safety in that feeling of control. But, that sense of safety is completely contrived. To truly believe that you can get someone else to do something solely through your own manipulation means to suspend the wholeness of another. Taking credit for total control means ignoring all the other ways the object of manipulation is being controlled—socially, financially, internally beyond their knowledge even. To live in truth means understanding the limits of one’s control over the internal and external. It means facing the disorder, the random, the unfair that exists in truth.
