Waiting for Godot

April 13, 2025

Written for Existentialism at Duke, Spring 2025.

I was struck by the introduction of forgetting in Act 2 of Waiting for Godot. As a reader, it created an almost dream-like warping of the reality in the scenes. It honestly confused me. Where it was easy to visualize the simple setting and interactions between characters, the “forgetting” added a supernatural effect. In my confusion, I thought about the role of memory and forgetting in our relations with others. In interactions with peripheral people in my life, I usually do not take offense if they do not remember me from one or two inconsequential interactions. In fact, it happens a lot, so I’m used to it. I used to be in the habit of telling someone that I actually remembered them, even if they didn’t remember me, and detailing the interaction we had. I thought that it was a nice thing to do because it communicated that they made an impression on me. But when I told this to my friends, they said it probably made people uncomfortable because they couldn’t reciprocate. I found the politeness and nuances around forgetting and remembering someone interesting. The fact that it is such a taboo to forget someone’s name or details speaks to the fact that it holds a value. Some people take it as a deep personal offense when someone doesn’t remember them. In the play, Vladimir was seriously disturbed when no one remembered him from the day before, even that Estragon didn’t remember their time together. It pokes a sore spot where we need ourselves to live in others’ memories to validate our existence and experiences, where being seen in the eyes of the other is a fundamental aspect of our self-worth.
